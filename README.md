# TimestampCam

An iPhone video recorder that burns a millisecond-resolution wall-clock
timestamp into the top-right corner of every frame.

```
16:20:08:798 LOCAL
23:20:08:798 UTC
```

Built because a stock iPhone recording cannot give you this. Its only absolute
time field is `com.apple.quicktime.creationdate`, which stores whole seconds and
nothing finer, and its frame spacing is variable — run `movtime.py` against any
`.MOV` from the camera roll to see both for yourself.

## How the clock stays accurate

Two clocks, one anchor.

**Frame time** comes from `CMClockGetHostTimeClock()`, the monotonic clock
AVFoundation stamps capture buffers with. It is applied near sensor readout, so
a slow delegate or a busy main thread cannot corrupt it, and NTP cannot step it.

**The UTC anchor** is measured by a small SNTP client (RFC 4330, UDP/123) that
samples Apple, Cloudflare and pool.ntp.org several times each and keeps the
exchange with the smallest round-trip. That min-delay filter is what real NTP
implementations use: asymmetric queueing delay is the dominant error term and it
correlates with total delay, so the fastest exchange is the most trustworthy.
T1 and T4 are read from the host clock, not `Date()`, so the offset lands
directly in the domain the overlay needs.

The anchor is captured before recording and **frozen for the clip**:

```swift
frameTime = unixAtAnchor + (framePTS - hostAtAnchor)
```

Nothing on the per-frame path calls `Date()` or touches the network. When a
phone that has been offline reacquires service, iOS steps its wall clock —
sometimes by hundreds of milliseconds, sometimes backwards. With a frozen
anchor that step cannot reach the frames, and timestamps stay monotonic.

## Cost per frame

One subtraction and one addition for the time, then a blend of pre-rasterised
glyph bitmaps into roughly a 400×150 corner of the luma plane — about 40 KB
touched out of a 3 MB frame.

The camera's native 4:2:0 YCbCr buffer is passed to the encoder untouched apart
from that corner. Requesting BGRA instead would force a full-frame colour
conversion on every frame purely to make drawing convenient. This is why the
digits are white: we write the brightness plane and neutralise chroma over the
plate. **The video itself is full colour and pixel-identical to a stock
recording** — only the glyphs are monochrome.

The app shows a live `µs/frame` readout so you can confirm the cost on your own
device rather than taking this file's word for it.

## Building and installing

No Xcode needed locally. GitHub Actions builds it.

1. Push to GitHub. The workflow runs on every push.
2. Open the run, download the **TimestampCam-ipa** artifact, unzip it.
3. Install `TimestampCam.ipa` with [Sideloadly](https://sideloadly.io) or
   AltStore, signing with your Apple ID.

The `.xcodeproj` is generated by XcodeGen from `project.yml` during the build,
so there is no project file in the repo to drift out of sync.

A free Apple ID signature expires after 7 days and the app stops launching until
you re-sideload. A paid developer account issues year-long certificates.

## Verifying it

1. Run `python3 msclock.py` on a Mac, fullscreen, large font.
2. Check your Mac's offset at [time.is](https://time.is) — it reports this to
   the millisecond, round-trip corrected.
3. Record the screen with TimestampCam for a minute or more.
4. Step through frames and compare the burned-in label against the filmed clock.

**Reading the result:** you are not looking for zero. A constant offset is fixed
capture-pipeline latency plus display lag, and it calibrates out. What matters is
whether it stays constant. Growing means drift; jumping means a step got through.

Your monitor refreshes roughly every 17 ms, which caps this test at about
±20–30 ms no matter how good the sync is. It will catch timezone errors, whole-
second offsets, steps and drift. It will not confirm millisecond-grade absolute
accuracy — that needs an LED reference driven by a GPS-disciplined source.

## Known limits

- **Holdover drifts.** The last good anchor is persisted so a recording made
  with no signal still has a measured reference, but the oscillator's rate error
  is not learned — expect roughly a second of error per day since the last sync.
  The clock badge shows the anchor's age. Sync before heading out.
- **No independent offline time source.** iOS exposes no raw GNSS clock, so
  there is nothing to sync against with no network. Never synced at all means
  the clip is anchored to iOS's own clock and stamped `UNVERIFIED`.
- **Rolling shutter.** Rows are exposed sequentially, so one timestamp per frame
  is an approximation across the frame height.
- **Exposure window.** At 1/120 s the frame represents an ~8 ms window, not an
  instant.
- **Fixed frame rate.** Frame duration is pinned to 60 fps (or 30 where 60 is
  unavailable) for predictable timing. This costs exposure in dim scenes; relax
  `lockFrameRate` in `CaptureController.swift` if that matters more.
