# TimestampCam

An iPhone video recorder that burns a millisecond-resolution wall-clock
timestamp into the top-right corner of every frame.

```
16:20:08:798 LOCAL
23:20:08:798 UTC
```

Built because a stock iPhone recording cannot give you this. Its only absolute
time field is `com.apple.quicktime.creationdate`, which stores whole seconds and
nothing finer, and its frame spacing is variable — run `movtime.py` against any
`.MOV` from the camera roll to see both for yourself.

## How the clock stays accurate

Two clocks, one anchor.

**Frame time** comes from `CMClockGetHostTimeClock()`, the monotonic clock
AVFoundation stamps capture buffers with. It is applied near sensor readout, so
a slow delegate or a busy main thread cannot corrupt it, and NTP cannot step it.

**The UTC anchor** is measured by a small SNTP client (RFC 4330, UDP/123) that
samples Apple, Cloudflare and pool.ntp.org several times each and keeps the
exchange with the smallest round-trip. That min-delay filter is what real NTP
implementations use: asymmetric queueing delay is the dominant error term and it
correlates with total delay, so the fastest exchange is the most trustworthy.
T1 and T4 are read from the host clock, not `Date()`, so the offset lands
directly in the domain the overlay needs.

The anchor is captured before recording and frozen at record start:

```swift
frameTime = unixAtAnchor + (framePTS - hostAtAnchor)
```

Nothing on the per-frame path calls `Date()` or touches the network. When a
phone that has been offline reacquires service, iOS steps its wall clock —
sometimes by hundreds of milliseconds, sometimes backwards. With a frozen
anchor that step cannot reach the frames, and timestamps stay monotonic.

Three safeguards keep the anchor honest:

- **Sleep.** The host clock stops while the device sleeps. The anchor records
  how long the device had slept when it was measured (`mach_continuous_time`
  minus the host clock) and shifts itself by any sleep since, so an anchor
  measured before the phone was locked is still exact after.
- **Resync.** The app re-measures at launch, on returning to the app, and
  every minute while idle. During a recording it re-measures every minute and
  eases each correction in over 10 seconds, so drift can't build up over a
  long game and timestamps never jump or run backwards.
- **Cross-check.** At record start the anchor is compared with the device's
  own clock. They normally agree to tens of ms; if they differ by more than a
  second the clip uses the device clock instead and is stamped `UNVERIFIED`.

## Cost per frame

A subtraction, an add and a clamp for the time, then a blend of pre-rasterised
glyph bitmaps into roughly a 400×150 corner of the luma plane — about 40 KB
touched out of a 3 MB frame.

The camera's native 4:2:0 YCbCr buffer is passed to the encoder untouched apart
from that corner. Requesting BGRA instead would force a full-frame colour
conversion on every frame purely to make drawing convenient. This is why the
digits are white: we write the brightness plane and neutralise chroma over the
plate. **The video itself is full colour and pixel-identical to a stock
recording** — only the glyphs are monochrome.

The app shows a live `µs/frame` readout so you can confirm the cost on your own
device rather than taking this file's word for it.

## Building and installing

No Xcode needed locally. GitHub Actions builds it.

1. Push to GitHub. The workflow runs on every push.
2. Open the run, download the **TimestampCam-ipa** artifact, unzip it.
3. Install `TimestampCam.ipa` with [iLoader](https://github.com/nab138/iloader)
   (**Import any IPA**), signing with your Apple ID.
4. On the phone: Settings > General > VPN & Device Management > trust your
   Apple ID, and Settings > Privacy & Security > Developer Mode > on.

Sideloadly 0.60 does not work: since September 2026 Apple rejects the Xcode
login identity it uses (HTTP 503 from `gsa.apple.com`), which it reports as
"Guru Meditation … Invalid file".

The `.xcodeproj` is generated by XcodeGen from `project.yml` during the build,
so there is no project file in the repo to drift out of sync.

A free Apple ID signature expires after 7 days and the app stops launching until
you re-import it in iLoader. A paid developer account issues year-long
certificates.

Long recordings are safe to leave running: the screen stays on while
recording, leaving the app or a call stops and saves the clip, and files are
written in 10-second chunks so a crash loses at most the last chunk (the next
launch saves whatever was left behind to Photos).

## Verifying it

1. Run `python3 msclock.py` on a Mac, fullscreen, large font.
2. Check your Mac's offset at [time.is](https://time.is) — it reports this to
   the millisecond, round-trip corrected.
3. Record the screen with TimestampCam for a minute or more.
4. Step through frames and compare the burned-in label against the filmed clock.

**Reading the result:** you are not looking for zero. A constant offset is fixed
capture-pipeline latency plus display lag, and it calibrates out. What matters is
whether it stays constant. Growing means drift; jumping means a step got through.

Your monitor refreshes roughly every 17 ms, which caps this test at about
±20–30 ms no matter how good the sync is. It will catch timezone errors, whole-
second offsets, steps and drift. It will not confirm millisecond-grade absolute
accuracy — that needs an LED reference driven by a GPS-disciplined source.

## Known limits

- **Offline holdover drifts.** With no signal, a recording uses the last good
  anchor and can't make its per-minute corrections, so it drifts at the
  crystal's own rate — up to about a second per day since the last sync. The
  clock badge shows the anchor's age.
- **No independent offline time source.** iOS exposes no raw GNSS clock, so
  there is nothing to sync against with no network. Never synced at all means
  the clip is anchored to iOS's own clock and stamped `UNVERIFIED`.
- **Rolling shutter.** Rows are exposed sequentially, so one timestamp per frame
  is an approximation across the frame height.
- **Exposure window.** At 1/120 s the frame represents an ~8 ms window, not an
  instant.
- **Frame rate.** `lockFrameRate` asks for 60 fps, but recordings so far come
  out at about 29.6 fps, so the lock is not taking effect. Timestamps are
  unaffected: every frame carries its own time.

## Mac window recorder

`timestampcap` records one or more Mac windows with the same burned-in
timestamp, using the same `Core/` code as the iPhone app: host-clock frame
times, SNTP anchor frozen per recording, luma-plane overlay.

It builds with plain Command Line Tools, no Xcode. See **Commands** below.

## Files

| Path | Role |
|---|---|
| `Core/HostClock.swift` | Monotonic clock and boot identity (shared) |
| `Core/SNTPClient.swift` | NTP client with min-delay selection (shared) |
| `Core/TimeAnchor.swift` | The frozen host→UTC mapping, and its store (shared) |
| `Core/TimestampOverlay.swift` | Glyph atlas and luma-plane burn-in (shared) |
| `Core/ClipClock.swift` | Per-recording clock with eased per-minute corrections (shared) |
| `MacCapture/main.swift` | Mac recorder: clock sync, window picker, status line |
| `MacCapture/WindowRecorder.swift` | Mac recorder: one window's capture and writer |
| `TimestampCam/CaptureController.swift` | Session, writer, frame loop |
| `TimestampCam/ContentView.swift` | Record button, REC badge, clock status |
| `build_mac.sh` | Builds the Mac recorder |
| `frame_offsets.py` | Reads both timestamps off every frame and plots the offsets |
| `requirements.txt` | Python packages for `frame_offsets.py` |
| `movtime.py` | Dumps every time field in a `.MOV` |
| `msclock.py` | Millisecond wall clock for filming |

## Commands

Run everything from the project folder.

### Mac window recorder

```
./build_mac.sh                        # build (once, and after code changes)
.build/timestampcap                   # record; saves to the current folder
.build/timestampcap /Volumes/Lexar    # record; saves to the flash drive
```

It syncs the clock, lists open windows by number, and asks which to record
(e.g. `1,3`). Each window becomes its own `.mov`. Press **Enter** or
**Ctrl-C** to stop; both finish the files properly.

- macOS records **windows, not browser tabs**: drag each tab you want into its
  own window first.
- First run only: System Settings > Privacy & Security > Screen Recording >
  turn on your terminal app, then quit and reopen the terminal.
- A **FAT32** drive can't hold a file over 4 GB, which a long recording of a
  busy window will exceed. Format the drive as **exFAT** (Disk Utility > Erase)
  before recording long sessions to it.

### Frame time analyzer

```
uv pip install -r requirements.txt                  # once
uv run msclock.py                                   # the clock to film / record
uv run frame_offsets.py PHONE.MOV                   # phone clip vs the clock it filmed
uv run frame_offsets.py PHONE.MOV MAC.mov           # phone vs Mac recording
uv run frame_offsets.py PHONE.MOV MAC.mov --redo    # re-read the videos, ignore the cache

# Long recordings: read every 10th frame instead of all of them
uv run frame_offsets.py /Volumes/Lexar/twenty-min-capture.mov --every 10
```

Reading takes very roughly 0.1-0.3 s per frame, so a 20-minute recording
(~35,000 frames) takes hours to read in full. `--every 10` cuts that tenfold
and still leaves ~80 readings in every 30-second running-mean window. Files on
another drive work the same way: give the full path, and the cache and plot are
written next to the video.

Record `msclock.py` with TimestampCam (and, for the comparison, with
`timestampcap` at the same time), then point the analyzer at the files. Use a
wildcard for long names, e.g. `Terminal-*1790023351.mov`.

It samples the start of each clip first and stops within seconds if it can't
read both timestamps, instead of failing after the full read. Readings are
cached as `<clip>.offsets.csv`, so re-plotting is instant. The plot is saved
as `<clip>.offsets.png`, or `<phone clip>.comparison.png` when comparing.

Each series is drawn three ways: individual frames as dots, a 30-second
running mean as a solid line, and a best-fit line dashed.

The comparison plots three lines:

- **Phone timestamp − Mac timestamp**, at the moment both show the same clock
  value. This is the phone-vs-Mac agreement check. It also includes the
  display's delay: the Mac captures each image before it reaches the screen,
  the phone films it after, so this reads high by that delay (typically tens of
  ms) even when both clocks are perfect.
- **Phone timestamp − clock the phone filmed**: camera plus display delay.
- **Mac timestamp − clock the Mac recorded**: rendering delay only, no camera
  or screen involved.
